The Quiet Power of Negative Space in Contemporary Interiors

Clara Nguyen · April 3, 2025 9 min read

In the rush to fill our homes with furniture, art, and objects, we often overlook the most powerful design tool of all: emptiness. Negative space—the area around and between things—is not wasted space. It is the breathing room that allows a room to feel composed, intentional, and alive.

Walk into a beautifully designed Japanese ryokan or a mid-century modern living room by a master like Finn Juhl. You will notice that the objects are few, but their placement is precise. The space between them is as considered as the objects themselves. This is the essence of negative space: it gives weight to what remains.

Why We Fear Emptiness

Our resistance to empty space is often psychological. An empty corner can feel unfinished, even lonely. We fill it with a plant, a side table, a floor lamp—sometimes all three. But in doing so, we dilute the impact of each element. When everything is emphasized, nothing is.

“Empty space is not empty. It is full of potential, of light, of movement.” — Anonymous architect

In spatial planning, negative space serves three functions: circulation, visual relief, and focal amplification. A generous hallway is negative space that makes movement comfortable. A blank wall above a sofa is negative space that lets a single artwork sing. A bare floor around a dining table is negative space that allows the table to be the undeniable centrepiece.

A single chair and lamp against a blank wall—negative space as luxury.

How to Use Negative Space Intentionally

Start by editing. Remove one object from every surface. Then remove another. Step back. Does the room feel more coherent? Often, the answer is yes. Next, consider the “visual weight” of each item. A dark, heavy sofa anchors a room; a light, airy chair does not. Balance heavy objects with open space, not with more objects.

Finally, use light as a material. A shaft of sunlight on an empty wall is a design feature. A dark corner can be intentional if it creates depth. Negative space is not about minimalism for its own sake—it is about clarity of intention.

The most memorable interiors are not the ones with the most stuff. They are the ones where every element has room to be itself. That is the quiet power of emptiness.

Lime Plaster and the Art of Impermanence

Isabelle Fournier · March 28, 2025 11 min read

Lime plaster is one of the oldest building materials in the world—and one of the most relevant today. Unlike modern gypsum or cement renders, lime plaster is breathable, flexible, and develops a patina over time. It does not fight against age; it embraces it.

Walk through a restored farmhouse in Provence or a contemporary gallery in Mexico City, and you will likely encounter walls finished in lime. The surface is never perfectly uniform. It shifts with the light, revealing subtle trowel marks and mineral variations. This imperfection is precisely the point.

What Is Lime Plaster?

Lime plaster is made from calcium hydroxide (slaked lime), mixed with water, sand, and sometimes natural pigments. When applied to a wall, it cures by absorbing carbon dioxide from the air, slowly turning back into limestone. This process—called carbonation—can take months or even years, during which the wall remains slightly breathable.

This breathability is crucial. It allows moisture to evaporate rather than become trapped, preventing mould and improving indoor air quality. In old buildings, lime plaster was the norm because it worked with the building’s movement, not against it.

“Lime plaster is a living finish. It records the hand of the maker and the passage of time.” — Marta Nowak, conservation architect

Lime plaster develops a unique patina, shifting with the light.

Why It’s Making a Comeback

As designers seek alternatives to high-carbon materials like cement, lime plaster offers a lower-energy option. It can be made locally, applied by hand, and eventually returned to the earth. Its aesthetic—soft, matte, and subtly variegated—also aligns with the current taste for natural, tactile interiors.

In modern homes, lime plaster is used on feature walls, fireplaces, and even ceilings. It pairs beautifully with timber, stone, and raw metals. Because it is alkaline, it also resists mould naturally, making it ideal for bathrooms and kitchens when properly sealed.

Practical Considerations

Lime plaster is not a quick fix. It requires skilled application, and curing times can be long. It is also more expensive than conventional paint. But for those who value longevity, breathability, and a finish that improves with age, it is an investment worth considering.

As we move toward more sustainable and human-centred design, lime plaster reminds us that impermanence is not a flaw—it is a feature. Our buildings can age gracefully, just as we do.

Layered Light: A Practical Guide to Residential Illumination

Elena Marchetti · March 20, 2025 8 min read

Most homes are under-lit in the wrong ways and over-lit in the wrong places. A single ceiling fixture in the centre of a room creates harsh shadows and flat, unflattering light. The solution is not more fixtures—it is better layers.

Professional lighting designers think in four layers: ambient, task, accent, and decorative. Each has a distinct role. When combined thoughtfully, they create spaces that feel both functional and atmospheric.

Layer 1: Ambient Lighting

Ambient light provides overall illumination. Instead of a single central fixture, distribute ambient light across the ceiling: recessed downlights, cove lighting, or wall sconces. Aim for a soft, even wash of light that eliminates dark corners without glaring.

Layer 2: Task Lighting

Task lighting is directed where you need it: reading, cooking, working. This includes desk lamps, under-cabinet kitchen lights, and bedside reading sconces. The key is adjustability—articulated arms, dimmers, and focused beams.

Layer 3: Accent Lighting

Accent lighting adds drama and highlights architectural features or objects. Think picture lights, track lighting aimed at a sculpture, or LED strips behind a headboard. Use accent light sparingly—it is the spice, not the meal.

Layer 4: Decorative Lighting

The fourth layer is the fixture itself as a design object: a sculptural pendant over a dining table, a vintage chandelier in an entryway. Decorative lighting should be chosen for its form as much as its light output. It is the jewellery of the room.

Layered lighting creates depth and warmth in a living room.

Colour Temperature and Dimming

Always use dimmers. And pay attention to colour temperature: 2700K–3000K for living spaces (warm white), 3000K–3500K for kitchens and bathrooms (neutral), and 4000K+ only for garages or utility areas. Mixing temperatures in the same room is a common mistake—keep it consistent.

Finally, consider how light changes throughout the day. A good lighting plan allows you to adjust from bright and energising in the morning to warm and dim in the evening. This supports circadian rhythm and makes your home feel more alive.

Layered lighting is not complicated, but it requires intention. Start by auditing your current lighting. Where are the shadows? Where is the glare? Then add layers one at a time. Your eyes—and your mood—will thank you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common queries about interior design, architecture, and working with our editorial content.

What is the difference between interior design and interior architecture? +
Interior design focuses on finishes, furnishings, and spatial layout within existing walls. Interior architecture involves modifying the structure itself—moving walls, adding openings, or designing built-in elements. Both are essential to a holistic project.
How do I choose the right lighting for a small apartment? +
Prioritise layered lighting with dimmers. Use wall sconces and floor lamps to avoid ceiling clutter. Choose warm colour temperatures (2700K–3000K) and consider mirrored surfaces to bounce light around.
Are natural materials always more sustainable? +
Not automatically. Sustainability depends on sourcing, transport, and longevity. Local, responsibly harvested timber and low-carbon lime plaster are often excellent choices, but every material has a lifecycle impact.
What is the ideal ceiling height for a comfortable room? +
For living spaces, 2.7–3.0 metres is comfortable. Below 2.4 metres can feel oppressive; above 3.5 metres may feel cavernous without vertical elements or layered lighting to bring it down to human scale.
How can I make a rental apartment feel like home without major renovations? +
Focus on lighting (swap out harsh fixtures with plug-in sconces), textiles (rugs, curtains), and removable wallpaper or freestanding storage. Small changes to hardware and art placement also have a big impact.
